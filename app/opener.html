Graham Duncan couldn’t focus on much except the pain.

This story is best experienced with sound.

The 62-year old was on the floor of his west-end Toronto home with a broken femur. Time blurred, but Duncan knew it had been a while since he dismounted terribly wrong from his bike; since his neighbour all but carried him to the door and since, in his initial surge of adrenaline, he scooted upstairs.

Now that adrenaline had worn off and moving again seemed impossible – certainly not downstairs, ruling out a car ride to the nearest hospital.

“If I could have gotten off the floor, I would have,” Duncan said.

The only thing he could do that September 2024 evening was wait for paramedics to arrive.

6:11 p.m.

Graham Duncan’s wife, Laurel, called 9-1-1.

Dispatcher: Toronto ambulance, where do you need us?

Laurel gave their west-end address, recounted how her husband got hurt, and notified the dispatchers that her husband was also a cancer patient. He wasn’t bleeding or throwing up, she told them – but he was in pain severe enough that he couldn’t stand up.

This is a problem that has besieged Toronto’s paramedic service for years, and played out in plain sight of decision-makers. Overstrain on Toronto emergency medical system has left patients waiting, sometimes for hours, in painful, uncomfortable and even dangerous situations.

Officials have acknowledged that something needs to change. In June 2025, days after bystanders waited on hold with 9-1-1 for nearly seven minutes after a 15-year-old boy was shot and lay waiting for help, Toronto Mayor Olivia Chow told reporters there would be a $52-million investment over the coming years to boost staffing and hasten responses:

“Torontonians deserve a paramedic service that is quick to respond in an emergency because people cannot wait.”

But that hasn’t happened.
