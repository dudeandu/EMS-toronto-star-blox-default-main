Graham Duncan couldn’t focus on much except the pain.

This story is best experienced with sound.

The 62-year old was on the floor of his west-end Toronto home with a broken femur. His wife Laurel was nearby, waiting for an ambulance to come help. Time blurred, but Duncan knew it had been a while since he dismounted terribly wrong from his bike; since his neighbour all but carried him to the door and since, in his initial surge of adrenaline, he scooted upstairs.

Now that adrenaline had worn off and moving again seemed impossible – certainly not downstairs, ruling out a car ride to the nearest hospital.

“If I could have gotten off the floor, I would have,” Duncan said.

6:11 p.m.

Graham Duncan’s wife, Laurel, calls 911 for the first time.

Dispatcher: Toronto ambulance, where do you need us? Caller: Hi, 582 Dovercourt Road. Dispatcher: Just please repeat that for verification? Caller: 582 Dovercourt Road. Dispatcher: And what’s the closest major intersection over there? Caller: Uh, Bloor and Ossington.

The only thing he could do that September 2024 evening was wait for paramedics to arrive. But hours ticked by – one, two, three, then four.

7:08 p.m. 57 minutes since the initial call

Laurel calls 911 again.

Dispatcher: Toronto ambulance, where do you need us? Caller: Hi, well I actually called about an hour ago, and I just wondered if there was any sense of when they might come.

Dispatcher: Okay, let me double check your call for you. What’s the address you gave? Caller: The address is 582 Dovercourt Road. Dispatcher: May I ask who I’m speaking with? Caller: Laurel.

Dispatcher: Unfortunately, Laurel, there is still a delay. We don’t have any available ambulances just yet, but as soon as we have one available, we’ll be sending it to you.

Dispatcher: Is there any serious bleeding? Caller: There’s no bleeding. He’s just in severe leg pain. No bleeding, it’s internal pain.

Caller: We called the ambulance because he’s not able to stand up, and we’re on the second floor. Dispatcher: Is he still responding normally? Caller: Yes, he’s awake, he’s conscious.

Dispatcher: As soon as we have an ambulance available, we’ll be sending it to you. If anything changes, call us back at 911 for further instructions. Caller: Okay, thank you.

Laurel called 9-1-1 three times, in those hours: her husband, who also had cancer, was in worsening agony and immobile on the floor. When would the paramedics be able to help?

This is a problem that has besieged Toronto’s paramedic service for years, and played out in plain sight of decision-makers. Overstrain on Toronto emergency medical system has left patients waiting, sometimes for hours, in painful, uncomfortable and even dangerous situations.
